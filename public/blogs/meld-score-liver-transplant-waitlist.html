What is a MELD Score? How It Affects Your Position on the Transplant Waitlist

What is a MELD Score? How It Affects Your Position on the Transplant Waitlist

05 Sep 2025

As a liver transplant surgeon in Delhi, I’ve sat with thousands of patients and their families. I understand that a diagnosis of end-stage liver disease brings a storm of questions, fears, and complex medical terms. Of all these terms, none carries more weight than the MELD score.

The Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) score is a number calculated from your blood tests. It objectively measures how sick your liver is and predicts your risk of mortality in the next three months, determining your priority on the transplant waitlist.  

But to you, it’s so much more than a number. It’s a source of anxiety, a measure of hope, and the key that can unlock a second chance at life. My goal with this guide is to demystify the MELD score. I want to walk you through what it is, how it’s calculated, and—most importantly—what it truly means for your journey ahead. This isn’t just medical information; it’s your roadmap.


What is the MELD Score and Why Does It Exist?

Before 2002, the system for allocating deceased donor livers was different. It relied heavily on how long a patient had been waiting. While that sounds fair on the surface, it meant that someone who was relatively stable could receive an organ before someone who was critically ill, simply because they had been on the list longer.

The medical community knew there had to be a better, more equitable way. This led to the nationwide adoption of the MELD system.  

The core purpose of the MELD score is simple but profound: to ensure that the sickest patients get the highest priority for a transplant. It’s a system designed to save the most vulnerable lives first, based on objective, verifiable medical data.  

The score itself is a numerical scale that typically ranges from 6 to 40 :  

  • A score of 6 indicates the least severe illness or near-normal liver function.
  • A score of 40 indicates the most severe, life-threatening illness.

Your MELD score is a dynamic number that predicts your statistical risk of dying from liver disease within the next three months. A higher score means a higher risk, and therefore, a more urgent need for a transplant. This is why, in the complex world of organ allocation, a high MELD score moves you up the waitlist.  


How Your MELD Score is Calculated: A Look at the Four Key Labs

Your MELD score isn’t a subjective assessment. It’s calculated using a precise mathematical formula based on the results of four standard blood tests. In 2016, the formula was updated to include Serum Sodium, making it even more accurate. This is why you might hear it called the MELD-Na score.  

Let’s break down what each of these four tests tells us about your health.


Total Bilirubin

Bilirubin is a yellowish substance made during the body's normal process of breaking down old red blood cells. A healthy liver processes bilirubin and excretes it as bile. When your liver is damaged, it can’t clear bilirubin effectively, causing it to build up in your blood. This leads to jaundice (yellowing of the skin and eyes). A high bilirubin level is a direct indicator of poor liver function.  


International Normalized Ratio (INR)

Your liver produces vital proteins that help your blood to clot. The INR test measures how long it takes for your blood to form a clot. If your liver is failing, it can’t produce enough of these clotting factors. This means your blood will take longer to clot, resulting in a high INR. This test is a crucial measure of your liver’s synthetic function—its ability to make the things your body needs.  


Creatinine

You might be wondering why a test for kidney function is used to measure liver disease. The liver and kidneys are intimately connected. Severe liver disease, particularly cirrhosis, often leads to impaired kidney function—a condition known as hepatorenal syndrome. Including creatinine in the formula provides a more holistic picture of your overall health and gives a more accurate prediction of short-term mortality risk.  


Serum Sodium

Low sodium levels in the blood (hyponatremia) are a common and serious complication of advanced cirrhosis. It indicates a profound imbalance in your body's ability to regulate fluids, which is often linked to severe portal hypertension (high blood pressure in the veins that supply the liver). Adding sodium to the MELD score in 2016 significantly improved its accuracy in predicting which patients were the sickest.  

These four values are plugged into the MELD-Na formula, generating the score that defines your place on the waitlist.


Your Score in Motion: Updates, Changes, and What to Expect

One of the most important things for patients to understand is that your MELD score is not a one-time calculation. It’s a living number that changes as your condition changes. Your transplant team will order these lab tests regularly to ensure your score accurately reflects your current state of health.

The Emotional Rollercoaster of a Changing Score

This is a topic I discuss with every patient. The MELD score creates a difficult paradox that can be emotionally draining. On one hand, a rising score is terrifying; it’s a numerical confirmation that your health is getting worse. On the other hand, that same rising score brings a sense of hope, as it means you are moving closer to receiving a life-saving transplant.  

It is completely normal to feel conflicted. You may find yourself in the strange position of hoping to become "sick enough" to get a transplant. Please know that these feelings are valid. Acknowledge them, discuss them with your family and your medical team. My job, as the top liver transplant surgeon in Delhi, is not just to manage your physical health, but to support you through this immense psychological challenge.


Beyond the Basic Score: MELD Exceptions and Special Cases

The MELD system is incredibly effective for the vast majority of patients with chronic liver disease. However, there are certain medical situations where the four lab tests alone don't capture the true urgency of a patient's condition. For these cases, the system has built-in exceptions.  


PELD for Children

For children under the age of 12, a different but related scoring system is used: the Pediatric End-Stage Liver Disease (PELD) score. Like MELD, it uses bilirubin, INR, and creatinine. However, it also includes factors crucial to a child’s health, such as albumin levels, age, and whether the child is experiencing growth failure.  


When Your Score Doesn't Tell the Whole Story: MELD Exception Points

Some patients have conditions that carry a high risk of mortality but don't necessarily cause the four MELD lab values to become dramatically abnormal. The most common example is Hepatocellular Carcinoma (HCC), or primary liver cancer. A patient might have a large, dangerous tumour but a relatively low MELD score. Without an adjustment, they would be at a disadvantage on the waitlist.

To address this, transplant bodies like UNOS have established a system of "MELD exception points." Patients with specific conditions can be awarded a higher, standardized score to ensure they have a fair chance at transplantation.

Common conditions that may qualify for MELD exception points include :  

  • Hepatocellular Carcinoma (HCC)
  • Hilar Cholangiocarcinoma (a type of bile duct cancer)
  • Hepatopulmonary Syndrome
  • Portopulmonary Hypertension

  • Certain metabolic diseases


A Surgeon's Insight: How Exception Points are Actually Calculated

Patients often ask me how these exception scores are decided. It’s not an arbitrary number. The system is designed to be as fair as possible by tying the exception score to local transplant statistics.

In many systems, the standard exception score is calculated based on the median MELD score at transplant (MMaT) for hospitals within a certain radius. Let me simplify that. Imagine that in the last year, half the patients who received a transplant in your region had a score of 28 or lower, and half had a score above 28. The median score is 28. A standard exception might assign you a score of 25 (three points below the median). This ensures your priority is comparable to other patients in your area, giving you a realistic chance at an organ offer. 

 

The Highest Priority: Status 1A

There is a very rare and critical category known as Status 1A. This is reserved for patients with acute liver failure—a sudden, catastrophic loss of liver function in someone with no prior history of liver disease. These patients have a life expectancy measured in hours or days without a transplant and are given the absolute highest priority on the list.  


The "X-Factor" You Can't Ignore: How Your Location Affects Your Wait

This is one of the most difficult and least-understood aspects of the transplant system, and it’s something I believe every patient has a right to know. Your geographic location can have a profound impact on your wait time and outcome.


Why a MELD Score of 25 in Delhi Isn't the Same as in Mumbai

Organ allocation in India, as in many parts of the world, is managed on a regional basis. The country is divided into different zones for organ procurement and distribution. The stark reality is that the supply of donor organs and the number of patients waiting for them are not evenly distributed.  

This creates significant geographic disparity. A patient with a MELD score of 25 in a region with a high number of donors and fewer patients on the list might receive a transplant relatively quickly. Another patient with the exact same MELD score in a densely populated region with lower donation rates could wait much longer, facing a higher risk of becoming too sick for a transplant while they wait. Studies have shown that for the same MELD score, wait times can double and the risk of "waitlist dropout" can vary dramatically from one region to another.  


What Causes These Regional Differences?

Several factors contribute to this imbalance:

  • Organ Donation Rates: Public awareness and cultural factors can lead to very different organ donation rates between states.
  • Number of Transplant Centers: Some regions have more hospitals for liver transplant in Delhi, for example, than other areas, concentrating demand.
  • Population Density: Major metropolitan areas naturally have a larger pool of patients needing transplants.

Understanding this reality is crucial. It empowers you to have a more informed conversation with your surgeon about strategies like multi-listing (registering at transplant centers in different regions), which is permissible but has specific rules.  


The Other Pieces of the Puzzle: Blood Type, Body Size, and Time

While the MELD score is the primary driver of your position on the waitlist, it’s not the only factor. When a donor liver becomes available, a complex matching process begins. Here are the other critical criteria :  

  • Blood Type: This is the first and most basic compatibility check. A donor and recipient must have compatible blood types (O, A, B, or AB).
  • Body Size: The donor liver must be a suitable size for the recipient's body. A very large person cannot receive a very small liver, and vice versa.
  • Geographic Proximity: Organs are first offered to the sickest, MELD-matched patients within a local area before being offered to a wider region. This is to minimize the time the organ spends outside a human body.

  • Waiting Time: This is a common point of confusion. Since the MELD system was introduced, the amount of time you’ve spent on the list is not a primary factor. It is used only as a final tie-breaker if two patients in the same area have the exact same MELD score.  


Taking Control: Your Options When Your MELD Score is Low

One of the most frustrating situations for a patient is the "low-MELD dilemma." You may be suffering from debilitating symptoms of liver disease—fatigue, ascites (fluid buildup), confusion—but your lab tests result in a MELD score that is too low to be competitive on the deceased donor waitlist. You are sick, but you are not "sick enough" for the system.  

This is where it’s essential to know that you have another path.


The Lifesaving Alternative: Living Donor Liver Transplantation

Living donor liver transplantation is a remarkable procedure that allows a healthy person to donate a portion of their liver to a recipient. The liver has a unique ability to regenerate, and both the donor's and recipient's liver portions will grow back to full size within a few months.

The most significant advantage of a living donor transplant is that it completely bypasses the MELD-based waitlist system. You are not competing for a scarce resource. This offers several life-saving benefits:  

  • Timing: The surgery can be scheduled electively, allowing you to receive a transplant before your health deteriorates to the point of a very high MELD score.
  • Healthier Organ: The donor is a healthy individual who has undergone extensive medical evaluation, ensuring the organ is in optimal condition.
  • Better Outcomes: Receiving a transplant sooner, when you are stronger, often leads to a smoother recovery and better long-term survival rates.

If you have a low MELD score but are struggling with the quality of your life, exploring the option of a living donor is one of the most proactive steps you can take.


A Message From Your Surgeon: My Final Thoughts

The journey to a liver transplant is a marathon, not a sprint. The MELD score is your guide on that path, but it does not define you. It is a tool—a very important one—that we use to navigate a complex system.

Understanding this system is the first step. The next, and most important, is having an expert partner to guide you through it. The data, the scores, and the policies are all crucial, but they are no substitute for the relationship you build with your surgeon and your transplant team.

Whether you are just beginning this process, are waiting on the list, or are exploring living donation, you will have questions. You will have moments of fear and moments of hope. My commitment is to be there with you for all of them, providing not just the best surgical care available, but also the clarity, honesty, and support you and your family deserve.
