What is Jaundice?

Jaundice is not a disease in itself — it is a symptom, a yellow discolouration of the skin, whites of the eyes (sclera), and mucous membranes caused by an elevated level of bilirubin in the blood. Bilirubin is a yellow pigment produced when red blood cells break down. Normally, the liver processes bilirubin and excretes it through bile. When this process is disrupted — due to liver disease, bile duct obstruction, or excessive red blood cell breakdown — bilirubin accumulates in the body, causing the characteristic yellow tinge.

Jaundice is one of the most common reasons patients visit a liver specialist in India. It can range from a mild, self-limiting condition (such as in newborns) to a serious sign of significant underlying liver or biliary disease that requires urgent specialist evaluation and treatment.

Types of Jaundice

1. Pre-Hepatic (Haemolytic) Jaundice

Caused by excessive breakdown of red blood cells (haemolysis), producing more bilirubin than the liver can process. The liver itself is typically healthy.

Common causes:

  • Sickle cell anaemia
  • Thalassemia
  • Autoimmune haemolytic anaemia
  • Malaria

2. Hepatic (Liver) Jaundice

The most common type seen at a liver specialist clinic — caused by damage or disease within the liver itself that impairs its ability to process bilirubin.

Common causes:

  • Acute viral hepatitis (Hepatitis A, B, C, D, E)
  • Alcoholic hepatitis
  • Non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH)
  • Drug-induced liver injury (DILI) — paracetamol overdose, anti-tuberculosis drugs, herbal remedies
  • Autoimmune hepatitis
  • Liver cirrhosis from any cause
  • Acute liver failure

3. Post-Hepatic (Obstructive) Jaundice

Caused by blockage of the bile ducts outside the liver, preventing bile — and the bilirubin it contains — from draining into the intestine. This is a serious cause that often requires procedural or surgical intervention.

Common causes:

  • Gallstones (choledocholithiasis) — stones blocking the common bile duct
  • Pancreatic cancer — tumour compressing the bile duct
  • Cholangiocarcinoma (bile duct cancer)
  • Strictures (narrowing) of the bile duct
  • Pancreatic head tumours and periampullary cancers

4. Neonatal Jaundice

Extremely common in newborns, typically due to the immature liver's inability to process bilirubin rapidly enough. Most cases resolve without treatment. However, persistent or severe neonatal jaundice — particularly with pale stools — requires urgent paediatric liver evaluation to rule out biliary atresia.

Symptoms Associated With Jaundice

The yellow discolouration itself is the defining feature of jaundice, but accompanying symptoms provide important clues about the underlying cause:

Symptoms Suggesting Liver Cause

  • Fatigue and weakness
  • Nausea and loss of appetite
  • Fever — suggests acute hepatitis
  • Tender liver (on examination)
  • Dark urine, pale stools
  • Recent alcohol use or medication changes
  • History of hepatitis exposure

Symptoms Suggesting Bile Duct Blockage

  • Very dark urine (tea or cola-coloured)
  • Pale or clay-coloured stools
  • Severe itching (pruritus) — bile salts in skin
  • Pain in upper right or upper middle abdomen
  • Fever with chills (Charcot's triad — suggests cholangitis)
  • Recent onset with no prior liver disease
  • Weight loss (if cancer is the cause)
When to Seek Urgent Care:
If jaundice is accompanied by high fever, severe abdominal pain, confusion, or signs of bleeding, seek emergency specialist care immediately. These can indicate acute liver failure or ascending cholangitis — both potentially life-threatening.

How We Diagnose the Cause of Jaundice

Identifying the precise cause of jaundice is essential — because the treatment is entirely determined by the underlying diagnosis. Our systematic diagnostic approach leaves nothing to chance.

Blood Tests

  • Total and direct bilirubin levels — distinguishes between types of jaundice
  • Liver function tests (ALT, AST, ALP, GGT, albumin, INR)
  • Full blood count — detects haemolysis or infection
  • Viral hepatitis panel (Hepatitis A, B, C, D, E antibodies and antigens)
  • Autoimmune markers (ANA, ASMA, AMA) if autoimmune hepatitis is suspected

Imaging

  • Ultrasound abdomen — first-line imaging; detects dilated bile ducts, gallstones, liver texture changes
  • MRCP (Magnetic Resonance Cholangiopancreatography) — gold standard for visualising bile duct anatomy without invasive procedures
  • CT scan — useful for detecting tumours, lymph nodes, and liver lesions
  • ERCP (Endoscopic Retrograde Cholangiopancreatography) — used both for diagnosis and treatment of bile duct obstructions

Additional Tests When Needed

  • FibroScan — assesses degree of liver fibrosis
  • Liver biopsy — when a tissue diagnosis is required
  • Tumour markers (CA 19-9, CEA, AFP) — when cancer is suspected

Treatment for Jaundice — By Cause

Viral Hepatitis

Acute viral hepatitis (especially A and E) is usually self-limiting and managed with supportive care: rest, hydration, and avoidance of alcohol and liver-toxic drugs. Chronic Hepatitis B and C require antiviral therapies — see our hepatitis treatment page for details.

Alcoholic Hepatitis

Total alcohol abstinence is the most important. Treatment can include corticosteroids, nutritional supplements, and monitoring, depending on severity (Maddrey or MELD score). Severe cases with liver failure require transplant evaluation.

Drug-Induced Liver Injury (DILI)

Immediate withdrawal of the offending medication is essential. Recovery is typical within weeks. Anti-tuberculosis drug-induced jaundice is common in India and needs specialized care.

Obstructive Jaundice — Bile Duct Blockage

  • Gallstones: ERCP for stone removal, and laparoscopic cholecystectomy to prevent recurrence.
  • Bile duct strictures: Endoscopic dilation and stenting, or surgical reconstruction (hepaticojejunostomy).
  • Pancreatic or bile duct cancer: Surgical removal if feasible (Whipple or other), or palliative stenting if not.

Cirrhosis-Related Jaundice

Jaundice due to cirrhosis means worsening liver function. Management includes controlling fluid buildup (ascites), preventing/treating variceal bleeding and encephalopathy, and considering transplant evaluation for eligible patients.

Acute Liver Failure

Acute liver failure with jaundice is a medical emergency — requiring immediate hospital admission to a specialized Liver ICU. Supportive care is provided while the cause is treated; emergency transplantation may be life-saving.

Jaundice FAQs

Jaundice itself is a symptom — its danger depends entirely on the underlying cause. Mild jaundice from acute viral hepatitis in a young, otherwise healthy person is usually self-limiting. Jaundice in the setting of liver failure, bile duct cancer, or pancreatic cancer is a serious sign requiring urgent specialist attention.

Mild jaundice from viral hepatitis A or E can be managed at home with rest, adequate hydration, and avoidance of alcohol and hepatotoxic drugs — under specialist supervision and with regular blood test monitoring. Any jaundice with fever, severe abdominal pain, or confusion requires immediate hospital evaluation.

Duration depends on the cause. Acute viral hepatitis jaundice typically resolves within 2–6 weeks. Jaundice from bile duct obstruction will persist until the blockage is relieved. Jaundice in liver failure may be prolonged and indicates severe disease.

Patients with jaundice should eat small, frequent, easily digestible meals — avoiding fatty, fried, and spicy foods that stress the liver. Adequate hydration is important. Alcohol must be completely avoided. A diet rich in fruits, vegetables, and whole grains supports liver recovery.

For jaundice suspected to be liver-related, a dedicated liver specialist (hepatologist) offers the most comprehensive care — including advanced diagnostics (FibroScan, liver biopsy), antiviral treatment for hepatitis, and access to interventional and surgical procedures when needed.
